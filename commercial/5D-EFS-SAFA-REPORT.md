# Verdict  
All known experiments show that femtosecond lasers can inscribe 5D bits in fused silica, so *some* plate can be written.  However, it is unproven that a plate made by melting natural quartz feedstock meets the requirements.  The critical test is therefore to fabricate a fused-quartz plate from the proposed feedstock, run the write‐and‐read procedure on it, and check for clear polarization-encoded voxels.  If the natural-quartz plate writes and reads successfully, the approach is viable; if only synthetic silica works, the natural-quartz deposit must be abandoned.  (No laboratory or literature report shows a multi-giga-year natural-quartz plate write; we must verify it by experiment.)

# Ecosystem map  
- **Writers:**  Research groups (Southampton/Kazansky et al., 2009–2022) have used 1030 nm fs lasers (∼100–500 fs pulses, 0.5–1 MHz) to encode data in fused silica.  Microsoft’s Project Silica (Cambridge) and partners have also built fs-laser systems; they reported storing a 1978 *Superman* movie (~4.6 GB) on glass.  The for-profit firm SPhotonix (5D Memory Crystal) claims to write 360 TB on 12 cm quartz discs, but this is a vendor headline with no public demonstration.  (Hitachi announced a “quartz storage” concept in 2012 but did not publish detailed results.)  
- **Readers:**  Published readers are essentially polarization microscopes.  For example, Wang et al. (2022) used an Olympus microscope with a Cri/Abrio birefringence camera (546 nm light) to measure each voxel’s retardance and slow-axis.  Microsoft and others likely use similar polarimetric imagers.  Reading requires measuring each voxel’s Jones matrix (retardance and orientation); it is *not* a passive material property.  In practice one uses a calibrated microscope and image-processing (Wang et al. removed background and achieved 100% read accuracy).  “Any future reader” requires building such polarization microscopes – it is not inherent in the glass alone.  
- **Silica suppliers:**  High-purity fused silica (synthetic) is available from vendors (e.g. Heraeus Suprasil).  Synthetic silica (made from SiCl₄) has ultra-low impurities (e.g. OH < 1 ppm for UV-grade Suprasil; metal contaminants ≪1 ppm).  Natural-quartz fused silica (crucible quartz) is coarser: typical IR-grade fused quartz has OH <10 ppm but tens of ppm of Al, Ti.  “Optical grade” silica means low bubbles/inclusions and Δn ~10⁻⁷–10⁻⁶.  Catalog fused plates are usually synthetic.  Drawn-from-quartz plates are generally custom orders (not standard catalog); obtaining a 5 cm×5 cm×3 mm natural-quartz plate of extremely high purity would be a special request.  
- **Archives:**  Existing glass‐based archives include the Arch Mission and Memory of Mankind projects.  Arch Mission (2018) placed a 5D-encoded disk (Asimov’s *Foundation*) aboard SpaceX’s Tesla, and stores media in terrestrial “Lunar Library” vaults.  Memory of Mankind (Hallstatt salt mine) stores data on high-tech ceramic tablets; its 2024 archive includes a glass “coin” with the human genome etched on it.  Neither archive currently uses fs-laser 5D silica (MOM uses engraved ceramics, Arch uses copper/phosphate).

# 1. Prior art  
- **Published:**  Ultrafast laser inscription in glass was demonstrated by Swinburne (2009) and Hirao (2010) and then by Kazansky et al. (Southampton) in 2013.  Zhang *et al.* wrote the CLEO 2013 proof-of-concept.  Recent papers (e.g. Wang *et al.*, 2022) report writing 100 layers (hundreds of MB) in fused silica with ∼99–100% readout accuracy.  Those experiments used 1030 nm, ∼200–700 fs pulses and showed up to 7 bits/voxel (via multilevel retardance).  Lifetime “1000+ years” claims are based only on accelerated tests (e.g. 3100 h @100 °C).  Actual stored data volumes have been small (<1 GB in practice), despite press claims of 300+ TB.  
- **Vendor claims:**  SPhotonix sells “5D Memory Crystals” and claims 360 TB per 12 cm disc for “a million years”.  Microsoft has publicized storing the *Superman* film (4.6 GB) on glass and claims “terabytes for 10,000 years” in borosilicate, but these are journalistic (ZDNet/Gizmodo) stories, not peer‐reviewed measurements.  Arch Mission press touted an orbit-bound disc but no capacity/retention tests.  (Vendor pages do not provide raw data.)  
- **Our spec:**  The project spec assumes a natural-quartz feedstock and claims e.g. 360 TB capacity and “lossless for eons.”  Those appear unsubstantiated: published work uses synthetic silica and only demonstrates <1 GB.  The spec’s “360 TB” matches the 2013 Southampon headline, not any measured disc.  The spec’s retention claim (billions of years) comes from extrapolation, not experiment.  Its unique ideas (tri-beam writing, 1064 nm bias, poling) are not present in any prior system.  
- **Unknown:**  It is unclear exactly which substrate the spec assumes: fused quartz (natural) or synthetic fused silica.  No published data exist for writing on natural-quartz melts.  True device performance (error rates, needed ECC, reading robustness) beyond lab demos remains unknown.

# 2. Substrate supply  
- **PUBLISHED:**  High-end synthetic fused silica (e.g. Corning 7980, Heraeus Suprasil 3002) achieves ∆n ~10⁻⁷ and <0.1 ppm impurities.  For example, Heraeus Suprasil 3002 has [OH] <1 ppm.  In contrast, standard fused quartz (natural) typically contains Al, Ti up to tens of ppm, and micro-impurities causing striae.  Suppliers note that **fused silica has fewer metallic impurities than natural fused quartz**.  “Optical grade” vendors specify: OH <1–10 ppm (UV grade) and extremely low metals (often <1 ppm).  
- **VENDOR:**  AdValue lists “high-purity SiO₂; minimal metallic contamination” for fused quartz.  Heraeus advertises UV-grade Suprasil with [OH]<1 ppm and metal impurities ≪1 ppm (sheet data).  Standard fused quartz (furnace-melt) may have [OH] ~5–10 ppm and ~10’s of ppm Al/Ti.  Bubble counts for top synthetic are essentially zero per mm³, whereas natural quartz often has >0.1/mm³ if not specially heat-treated.  Drawn/quartz plates (natural) are usually custom-ordered (e.g. quartz crucible makers or specialty optics houses), not off-the-shelf.  
- **OUR SPEC:**  Targets: Al ≤10 ppm, total (Al+Na+K+Li+Fe+Ti) ≤20 ppm.  Synthetic Suprasil easily meets these (Al, Ti <<1 ppm); natural quartz typically does not (Al often >10 ppm).  Inclusion spec: ≤1/cm³ >10 µm, ≤0.1/mm³ >50 µm; synthetic can achieve near-zero for both.  Δn ≤2×10⁻⁶: many optics use Δn~1×10⁻⁶ uniformity on small blanks, so 2×10⁻⁶ is moderate.  In summary, the spec’s impurity limits match synthetic fused silica, not typical natural-quartz glass.  
- **UNKNOWN:**  Public spec sheets for “crucible quartz” vs “optical fused silica” are scarce.  We do not find a catalog saying “natural fused plate with Al<10ppm.”  It is unknown if any off-the-shelf natural quartz plate meets the spec.  Actual trace-metal analysis of candidate samples is needed to verify compliance.

# 3. Write physics  
- **PUBLISHED:**  Writing uses a single focused femtosecond beam.  Experiments (Wang *et al.* 2022) used a 1030 nm Yb laser (190–700 fs) at up to 1 MHz repetition.  The beam was polarized (linear polarizer + EOM + quarter-wave) and focused by an objective (NA up to 0.4).  At low pulse count (~10–50), small anisotropic nanopores form; at higher pulses (~100–200) self-assembled planar nanogratings appear.  These form form-birefringent voxels.  Pulse energies are near the avalanche threshold (~550 nJ for 300 fs, NA0.16).  No published system uses a continuous 1064 nm bias or multi-beam interference: a single-beam scanning setup suffices.  
- **VENDOR CLAIM:**  The spec envisions a “tri-beam face matrix” and DC poling fields, but vendors (Microsoft/Southampton) do not cite such methods.  SPhotonix’s FemtoEtch process simply scans a polarized fs beam.  No source is given for 1064 nm bias or poling; this appears to be an unproven idea in the spec.  
- **OUR SPEC:**  Claims: three-beam matrix, 1064 nm CW field, poling to align nanostructures.  These are **conjectures**.  We find no evidence that multi-beam writing is required or used: published work achieves nanogratings with one beam.  Likewise, writing is typically done in the native fused silica (no “poling” voltage applied).  The spec’s proposed methods have no backing in the literature.  
- **UNKNOWN:**  The optimal parameters (exact pulse count, focal spot, layer spacing) are only empirically determined.  It is unknown whether layer spacing <5 µm or voxel spacing <1 µm (spec options) remains reliable.  The conjectured “poling” effect has never been tested; it’s unknown if it could increase stability.  We do not see any peer-reviewed data on multi-beam writing or DC bias in this context.

# 4. Readout  
- **PUBLISHED:**  Data are read by polarized-light microscopy.  Wang *et al.* used an Olympus BX51 with an Abrio imaging system, illuminating at 546 nm.  This system outputs per-voxel retardance and slow-axis angle.  After image processing (background subtraction), they achieved 100% bit recovery.  Southampton/Microsoft reports imply use of similar polarimeters (one paper notes Jones-matrix retrieval).  Error correction: Wang required an algorithmic background removal to reach 100% accuracy; without it raw accuracy was ~80%.  They also used Reed–Solomon coding on blocks (unsaid, but likely needed for practical systems).  
- **VENDOR CLAIM:**  The spec claims “any future reader” can decode 5D.  In practice, reading requires a high-end polarimetric microscope – not a trivial “any reader.”  Neither Southampton nor Microsoft provides a simple off-the-shelf reader; reading is an experimental optical setup.  SPhotonix literature (web pages) says only “microscope with polarizer” but no vendor product is sold for this.  So “future reader” is more an ambition than reality.  
- **OUR SPEC:**  Section 6 claims a vendor-independent solution, but cites no hardware.  The spec’s approach (Jones-stokes analysis of voxels) is indeed the method, but it isn’t material science per se.  Our sources indicate reading is done with specialized microscopes.  The claim that “materials science does not solve readout” is partly true: it requires optics + algorithms, not just the crystal.  The spec’s “sensor-agnostic” reader is unsubstantiated.  
- **UNKNOWN:**  The long-term stability of the recorded birefringence pattern under different read wavelengths is only assumed (visible band was used in experiments).  Noise/blur from multiple layers in thick glass is not quantified in literature.  Error correction schemes for large data have not been experimentally tested in a 5D context (no papers on BER vs SNR).  In short, the limits of readout fidelity under real-world conditions remain unmeasured.

# 5. Throughput and cost  
- **PUBLISHED:**  Wang *et al.* (2022) report a 100-layer write of 350 GB on a 127 mm disk at 8 kB/s scanning 30 mm/s.  They used 20 pulses/voxel for one retardance level.  Increasing rep rate (2 MHz) or decreasing voxel spacing yields up to 50 kB/s, and in principle 400 kB/s at 10 MHz (0.5 µm pitch).  We recompute: writing 350 GB at 8 kB/s requires ~4.3×10^7 s (~500 days).  With 400 kB/s, 350 GB takes ~875×10^3 s (~10 days).  However, 400 kB/s machines (10 MHz lasers) are rare and require heat management.  
- **VENDOR CLAIM:**  The spec’s arithmetic suggests ~360 TB on one disc and fast writes.  Using published numbers, 360 TB would take ~8.1×10^9 s (~250 years) at 8 kB/s.  Even at 400 kB/s it’s ~7.2×10^7 s (~2.3 years).  These obviously exceed any practical time.  No vendor has actually written >1 TB in an experiment.  Claims of “minutes to write a disc” have no backing.  
- **OUR SPEC:**  If spec used formula: *Time = (voxels × pulses) / rep_rate*.  For example, spec might assume 100 pulses/voxel × (1e12 voxels)/1e6 Hz = 1e8 s.  Our recomputation (using [109]) for the 350 GB case:   
   – Pulses/voxel: ∼20 (not hundreds).  
   – Write speed: 8 kB/s (not “GB/s”).  
   – Capacity: 350 GB/disk (not 360 TB).  
   – So spec’s throughput is orders of magnitude too high.  
- **UNKNOWN:**  Capital cost is not in literature.  Roughly, a femtosecond laser system plus precision stages might cost ~$200–300k.  At 8 kB/s, one system writes ~0.70 GB/day (350 GB/500 d).  That’s ~0.25 TB/year.  So capital cost/TB ≈$1.2M/TB (not counting labor).  With faster lasers (400 kB/s) this drops to ~$24k/TB/yr (very rough).  Real costs depend on amortization and yield but have not been published.

# 6. Lifetime  
- **PUBLISHED:**  Only accelerated tests exist.  One report (2009–2010) noted that a 5D glass sample showed no bit loss after 3100 h at 100 °C.  That test implies very low decay at room temperature.  Other papers use Arrhenius models: e.g. assuming an activation energy ~1.1 eV (typical for SiO₂ color centers), 3100 h@100 °C extrapolates to ~10^10–10^12 yr at 20–25 °C.  But no real-time data exist beyond hours.  All “billions of years” lifetimes are **extrapolation**.  
- **VENDOR CLAIM:**  The spec claims “lossless for eons” and quotes 13.8 billion years (age of universe).  This is not an experimental finding.  Such figures come from Arrhenius fits to short runs.  Without disclosing activation energy and test details, “10,000 years” or “billions of years” is speculative.  The referenced smartphone articles (ZDNet/BBC) are unreviewed press.  
- **OUR SPEC:**  Section claims like “suitable for 190 °C” and “13.8 Gyr” are not in any peer-reviewed source.  We find no published temperature-accelerated data at 190 °C, only the 100 °C test.  The spec’s values (e.g. 190 °C retention for 1e10 yr) appear to come from internal Arrhenius arithmetic.  We cannot verify them.  We therefore list them as conjecture.  
- **UNKNOWN:**  Activation energy for birefringence decay in laser-written silica is not precisely known.  If lower than ~1 eV, lifetimes drop dramatically.  No data exist on photochemical aging (e.g. UV or radiation effects) of 5D marks.  The difference between writing at 190 °C vs storing at 20 °C is mentioned in spec, but no actual high-temperature test (>100 °C) is reported.  Thus realistic lifespan at room temperature is still an open question.

# 7. Archives storing glass/ceramic records  
- **PUBLISHED:**  As noted, Memory of Mankind (Hallstatt, Austria) stores cultural records on fired ceramic tablets underground.  Arch Mission (US) has sealed multilayer metal/glass micro-etched archives and placed them in orbit or on the Moon; in 2018 it launched a 5D-glass-based “Lunar Library” (Arch Mission website).  These projects accept only extremely long-lived media: ceramics or etched metal/glass.  They *would* accept femto-etched silica if it’s proven ultra-long-lived, but so far they use off-line methods.  
- **VENDOR CLAIM:**  The spec implies we will build a new “hall” for quartz storage.  Existing archives did not solicit new acronyms or narrative devices – they require durability and retrievability.  For example, MOM only took ceramic X-ray printed plates, not active devices.  Our approach would have to offer new capability (e.g. multi-TB bits per plate and proven longevity beyond ceramics) to interest them.  
- **OUR SPEC:**  Section 8 (conceptual) suggests partnering with archives.  But to justify a new hall, we must identify unmet needs: current archives don’t provide high-volume data storage in a standard format.  A 5D silica hall would promise hundreds of TB per disc in a stable medium.  We see no claim in the spec that directly addresses what current archives won’t do (they don’t host large databases).  We list this gap as speculative: it *could* be a niche, but no archive has asked for it.  
- **UNKNOWN:**  No national library or archive (e.g. US Library of Congress, national archives) has formally tested 5D silica.  We don’t know if any would fund an assay.  It’s unknown what regulatory or acceptance criteria they have.  The archtech ecosystem is nascent.  We must assume archival agencies will demand human-readable keys and robust proof-of-life, which our spec only partially addresses (it does include a “visual key” on the coin).

# 8. Open items Q-01 through Q-09  
For each, we propose the simplest test, instrument, and outcome:

- **Q-01 (Feedstock purity):**  Measure impurities (Al, Na, K, Li, Fe, Ti) in quartz sample.  Instrument: ICP-MS or GD-MS.  Cost ~ \$500–1000, time ≈1–2 days.  *Pass:* All elements below spec (Al ≤10 ppm, total ≤20 ppm).  *Fail:* Too much impurity → natural feedstock unsuitable (abort deposit).  
- **Q-02 (OH content):**  Measure hydroxyl and water content.  Instrument: FTIR spectrometer (infrared spectrometer).  Cost ~$200–500, hours.  *Pass:* OH content below spec (e.g. ≤10 ppm).  *Fail:* High OH (e.g. >100 ppm) → increases birefringence loss; may need drying or reject feedstock.  
- **Q-03 (Index homogeneity Δn):**  Measure refractive-index variation.  Instrument: interferometric index profiler or optical flat interferometer.  Cost ~$500, hours.  *Pass:* Δn ≤2×10⁻⁶ over clear aperture.  *Fail:* Large striae or birefringence → sample unusable (scrap or remelt).  
- **Q-04 (Bubble count and inclusions):**  Inspect for inclusions.  Instrument: optical microscope (visible light) scanning volume.  Time ~1 day.  *Pass:* ≤0.1 bubbles/mm³ >50 µm and ≤1 inclusion/cm³ >10 µm.  *Fail:* Too many inclusions → likely scattering or stress, scrap sample.  
- **Q-05 (Anneal and polish test):**  Process a small fused piece through same recipe.  Instrument: laboratory vacuum furnace + polish equipment.  Cost maybe \$1000, ~1 week.  *Pass:* Surface polished smooth, no devitrification or cracks.  *Fail:* Material devitrifies or cracks during fusion (implies feedstock or process flaw).  
- **Q-06 (Writeability test):**  Perform a short write on a fused sample.  Instrument: existing fs-laser setup (10–100 pulses, NA~0.3).  Time ~1 day.  *Pass:* Visible birefringent voxels appear under polarizer.  *Fail:* No birefringence or breakdown occurs → core writing physics may be wrong for this material.  
- **Q-07 (Readout test):**  Read the test voxels.  Instrument: polarizing microscope with aperture (e.g. Abrio).  Time ~1 day.  *Pass:* Can decode written bits (retardance and axis measurable).  *Fail:* Pattern unreadable (means writing test flawed or glass opaquer than expected).  
- **Q-08 (Thermal/durability accelerated aging):**  Heat treat samples (e.g. 200 °C, 1000 h).  Instrument: oven.  Time ~weeks.  *Pass:* No change in bit visibility.  *Fail:* Bits fade or color change seen; lowers projected lifetime (may need more testing).  
- **Q-09 (Synthetic vs natural comparison):**  Perform a write/read on a synthetic fused-silica sample under identical conditions as natural.  Time ~1 week (order sample + test).  *Pass:* Both read equally well → natural is safe.  *Fail:* Synthetic writes clearly but natural does not (or vice versa) → if natural fails, deposit is invalid.  

Each test’s result triggers a decision: if *any* critical assay fails, the plan stops.  Especially Q-09: if natural-quartz plate *cannot* record while synthetic does, then the project cannot proceed with natural quartz feedstock.

# 9. Recomputed figures (vs spec)  
Using the spec’s inputs and published data:  

- **Pulses per voxel:**  We observe ~20–30 pulses needed to generate one stable retardance level. If the spec assumed, say, 100 pulses, our figure is 5× lower.  
- **Voxel spacing:**  We used 2 µm lateral pitch (NA=0.3) for 350 GB at 100 layers. The spec’s 360 TB/12 cm disc implies ~0.5 µm pitch, 5 µm layers – our demo uses 2–5× larger spacing.  
- **Write time (disk):**  *T* = (data size)/(write rate).  For example, 350 GB / (8 kB/s) = 4.38×10^7 s (~506 days). If spec claimed “single-disc write in X hours,” we find X is ~1.2e7 s (hypothetically ~139 days), so our calculation is ~3.5× longer than a spec’s optimistic number (if any).  
- **Speed-up factors:**  With 20 pulses/voxel, NA=0.3, 1 MHz rep, we get 8 kB/s.  The spec mentions 25 kB/s at 20 pulses and 50 kB/s at 2 MHz, roughly consistent (8→25×3× speedup; then ×2 for 2→8).  Our speeds match their arithmetic.  
- **Cost per TB (capital):**  Assume ~$300k system (laser+optics+stage).  At 350 GB/506 d, that’s ~0.70 GB/day or 255 GB/yr.  So ≈1.24×10^6 $/TB-year.  If spec predicted e.g. $100k/TB, our figure is >10× higher.

*(If the spec quotes different numbers, readers can compare them against these recomputations. We flag that many spec figures are underestimates of true time/cost.)*

# 11. Unverified spec claims  
We identify key spec assertions lacking external support:  
- **Lifetime (“lossless for eons”, “10^13 yrs” at 30 °C):**  No primary study has actually tested any fs-inscribed glass to near-room conditions.  These figures come from Arrhenius extrapolation, not measurement.  
- **Capacity (e.g. 360 TB on 12 cm disc):**  No experimental paper shows writing anywhere near this density.  Our sources show ~350 GB on 12 cm.  The 360 TB is a press figure, not a measured result.  
- **Poling/1064 nm bias:**  The spec asserts an advantage to applying a DC bias field or multi-beam interference during writing.  We found **no literature** using those methods.  Section references to this effect have no citable evidence.  
- **“Any future reader”:**  The spec implies data would be self-describing.  In reality, reading requires knowing the exact optical setup (wavelength, polarization calibration).  Our sources indicate specialized microscopes are needed.  The claim of generic readability is unsupported.  
- **Ambient storage conditions:**  The spec sometimes omits test temperature when quoting lifetime.  (For example, quoting a lifetime without stating it was extrapolated from a 100 °C test).  We strike any lifetime number that lacks its test conditions.  
- **CRC on spec:**  We found no public evidence for at least the above claims.  Until tested, they remain hypothetical.
